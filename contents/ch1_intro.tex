\chapter{Introduction} \label{ch:introduction}

Quantum computing represents a paradigm shift in information processing by leveraging the fundamental principles of quantum mechanics, such as superposition and entanglement, to perform tasks that may be beyond the reach of classical computation. 
A primary objective has been to characterize the resources required to achieve speedup or advantage over classical computing. 
Shor’s algorithm~\cite{shor1997} for integer factorization, which achieves a superpolynomial speedup over the best known classical algorithm, and Grover’s algorithm~\cite{grover1996fastquantummechanicalalgorithm} for unstructured search, which achieves a quadratic speedup over the best possible classical strategy, are two foundational examples. 
Beyond computation, many central questions in quantum information theory can be naturally formulated as optimization problems. 
Given a quantum system, one may ask how much entanglement it contains, which measurement best extracts the information encoded in it, or what resources are required to learn a desired property from this system.

%Semidefinite programming is a subclass of convex optimization in which a linear objective function is optimized subject to linear constraints and positive semidefiniteness conditions.
%Since quantum states can be represented by positive semidefinite operators, measurements by collections of positive semidefinite operators that sum to identity, and many quantities can be expressed as linear functions optimized over these objects, it is natural to formulate several optimization problems in quantum information theory as instances of semidefinite programs.

The mathematical structure of quantum theory makes semidefinite programming~\cite{vandenberghe1996semidefinite, tunccel2016polyhedral} particularly well suited to addressing such questions. 
Semidefinite programming is a class of convex optimization problems, whose individual instances are called semidefinite programs, in which a linear objective function is optimized subject to linear constraints and positive semidefiniteness conditions. 
Quantum states are represented by positive semidefinite operators, while measurements are described by collections of positive semidefinite operators that sum to the identity. 
Moreover, many quantities of interest can be expressed as linear functions of these operators. 
Consequently, a broad range of optimization problems in quantum information theory admit natural formulations as semidefinite programs. 
This perspective has played a central role in the field, both as a computational framework and as a tool for establishing structural results~\cite{skrzypczyk2023semidefinite}.

%Several landmark results illustrate this. 
Jain, Ji, Upadhyay, and Watrous~\cite{jain2011qip} used the matrix multiplicative weights update method to approximate the optimal value of a semidefinite program, leading to the proof that QIP, the class of problems having quantum interactive proof systems, is equivalent to PSPACE, the class of problems decidable using polynomial space. 
More recently, Ji, Natarajan, Vidick, Wright, and Yuen~\cite{ji2021mip} proved that MIP\*, the class of multi-prover interactive proofs with entangled provers, equals RE, the class of recursively enumerable languages — a result implying, among other consequences, that the Navascu{\'e}s-Pironio-Ac{\'\i}n (NPA) hierarchy~\cite{navascues2007bounding} of semidefinite relaxations does not converge in general.
Together, these works demonstrate how semidefinite programming and related hierarchies provide a powerful language for resolving deep structural questions in quantum information.

Despite its power, the semidefinite programming perspective faces two major obstacles. 
The first is computational hardness: some natural structural constraints, most notably separability, are NP-hard to test in general~\cite{gurvits2004classical,gharibian2010strong}. 
The second is dimensionality: the Hilbert space of an $n$-qubit system has dimension $2^n$, so even optimization problems that are efficiently computable in principle may become computationally infeasible at scale. 
This dissertation studies several problems where these barriers arise and develops methods that exploit the underlying structure to reduce the effective dimension, enable hybrid quantum-classical computation, or provide provable guarantees for simpler measurement strategies. 
In particular, we employ semidefinite programming as both a theoretical framework and as a practical computational tool to investigate two areas of quantum information: the characterization of entanglement and the optimal discrimination of quantum states. 
The remainder of this chapter outlines specific problems addressed in this work, beginning with the challenge of identifying entanglement in bipartite systems.

\paragraph{Separability problem}

The first part of this thesis concerns the \emph{separability problem}. 
Entanglement is one of the defining features of quantum theory and a central resource in quantum information processing~\cite{horodecki2009quantum}. 
Given a bipartite quantum state, one asks whether it can be expressed as a convex combination of product states. 
States admitting such a decomposition are called \emph{separable}, while those that do not are said to be entangled. 
Determining whether a given state is separable is a fundamental problem in quantum information theory, but it is NP-hard in general~\cite{gurvits2004classical,gharibian2010strong}. 
Although one may consider separability or entanglement in multipartite systems, this dissertation focuses on the bipartite setting.

A variety of semidefinite programming relaxations have been developed to test separability. 
One of the most prominent is the Peres-Horodecki criterion, also known as the \emph{positive partial transpose} (PPT) condition, which states that every separable state remains positive semidefinite under partial transposition~\cite{peres1996separability, horodecki1996necessary}. 
The PPT condition is both necessary and sufficient for separability of $2 \times 2$ and $2 \times 3$ dimensional systems~\cite{horodecki1996necessary}, but in higher dimensions it is only a necessary condition. 
Other criteria, such as the realignment criterion~\cite{chen2003matrix,rudolph2005further}, and more systematic approaches, such as the Doherty-Parrilo-Spedalieri (DPS) hierarchy of symmetric extensions~\cite{doherty2002SymExt, doherty2004complete}, provide increasingly refined tests. 
In particular, the DPS hierarchy gives a sequence of semidefinite programming relaxations that converge to the set of separable states in the limit.

Despite its many applications~\cite{ioannou2007computational, brandao2011quasipolynomial, gharibian2013qma, chailloux2012complexity, hayden2013two, harrow2013testing, li2014quantum, consiglio2022variational, jeronimo2023power}, the separability problem remains difficult for two main reasons. 
First, even verifying that a proposed optimal solution is separable can be computationally hard. 
Second, the problem suffers from the curse of dimensionality: when the two subsystems are $n$-qubit spaces, the relevant Hilbert space has dimension $2^{2n}$, rendering direct numerical methods infeasible even for moderately large systems.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:Sep}, we address these obstacles by combining semidefinite programming techniques with quantum heuristics. 
By using semidefinite programming-based algorithms, one can solve small instances (i.e., when the dimensions of the two systems are small) with reasonable levels of success. 
We introduce a heuristic algorithm to reduce the dimension of the optimization problem, and discuss when this pre-processing step can be run on a quantum co-processor thereby alleviating the curse of dimensionality. 
In a sense, we do not need to perform any vector-represented calculations, the interactions are done on a quantum mechanical level and the desired quantities can be inferred from measurement statistics. 
Overall, this quantum-assisted pre-processing with semidefinite programming allows us to optimize over separable states in dimensions that would otherwise be inaccessible.

A key feature of our approach is that, although the algorithm is heuristic in nature, it always returns a feasible separable state.\footnote{This remains true even when the quantum processing component is noisy.} 
Thus, even in cases where the algorithm does not find an optimal solution,\footnote{Due to standard complexity-theoretic barriers $\mathrm{P} \neq \mathrm{NP}$, one should not expect any efficient method to solve all instances of the separability problem.} its output is still an explicit valid candidate solution, rather than merely a numerical bound.

As an application, we approximate the separable state that optimizes the energy with respect to a fixed Hamiltonian. 
We refer to this value as the \emph{separable ground energy}.\footnote{Since we formulate the relevant optimization problems as maximization problems, we regard the largest eigenvalue as the ground energy. This convention is without loss of generality, since one can replace $H$ by $-H$ if one prefers a minimization convention.} 
This approximation provides a way to quantify the amount of entanglement present in the ground space of the Hamiltonian. 
We demonstrate the method numerically for the one-dimensional Ising Hamiltonian on systems of up to $28$ qubits.

\paragraph{Quantum state discrimination}

The second part of this thesis studies several aspects of \emph{quantum state discrimination}~\cite{bae2015quantum}, one of the oldest and most fundamental tasks in quantum information processing. 
In its basic form, the task is to identify which state from a known set of candidate states was prepared. 
More formally, Alice has a finite set of quantum states $\{\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_N\}$, prepared with corresponding prior probabilities $\{p_1,\ldots,p_N\}$, and Bob is given a single state drawn from this set. 
His goal is to perform a measurement and guess the index of the state Alice sent. 
This simple two-party game, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_PBM-fig1}, underlies a wide range of problems in quantum information, including quantum key distribution~\cite{ko2018advanced}, two-party cryptography~\cite{aharonov2000quantum, van2002unambiguous, cabello2000quantum, ambainis2001new, sikora2017simple}, the hidden subgroup problem~\cite{hayashi2008quantum}, quantum algorithms~\cite{radhakrishnan2009random, hayashi2008quantum}, and dimension witnessing~\cite{strubi2013measuring}.

It is well known that Bob can identify the state perfectly if and only if the set of states are pairwise orthogonal~\cite{helstrom1969quantum}. 
Since this condition is often not satisfied, Bob must instead choose a figure of merit and optimize over all possible measurements. 
Two standard examples are minimum-error discrimination, in which Bob always outputs a guess and seeks to minimize the probability of error~\cite{helstrom1969quantum, holevo1973statistical, yuen2003optimum, bae2013structure}, and unambiguous discrimination, in which incorrect guesses are forbidden at the cost of allowing inconclusive outcomes~\cite{ivanovic1987differentiate, dieks1988overlap, peres1988differentiate}. 
%Other objectives are also possible, depending on the application, including state exclusion~\cite{bandyopadhyay2014conclusive} (which will be discussed shortly) and reward functions that assign partial credit to approximate or nearly correct answers. 
Other objective functions may also be appropriate, depending on the application. 
Examples include state exclusion~\cite{bandyopadhyay2014conclusive}, which will be discussed shortly, and reward functions that assign partial credit to approximate or nearly correct solutions.
We refer the reader to the surveys~\cite{chefles1998quantum, chefles2000quantum, bergou2004discrimination, bergou2007quantum, barnett2009quantum, bergou2010discrimination, bae2015quantum} for broader background.

A convenient way to unify these different tasks is through Helstrom's general Bayes framework~\cite{helstrom2003bayes}. 
In this formulation, Bob constructs a measurement with operators $\{M_1, \ldots, M_L\}$, where $L$ corresponds to the number of his possible guesses. 
A reward or penalty is assigned to each pair consisting of an input state and a measurement outcome. 
Thus, if $R_{ij}$ denotes the reward associated with guessing $i$ given that the received state is $\rho_j$, then Bob seeks to optimize an objective of the form
\begin{equation*}
    \sum_{i=1}^L\sum_{j=1}^N p_j R_{ij} \inner{M_i}{\rho_j},
\end{equation*}
subject to the constraint that $\{M_1, \ldots, M_L\}$ form a set of valid measurements. 
Different choices of the rewards $R_{ij}$ recover many familiar discrimination tasks. 
For an arbitrary ensemble, such optimization problems can be formulated as semidefinite programs~\cite{eldar2003designing, eldar2003semidefinite}. 
However, closed-form solutions are known only in special cases, such as discrimination of two states~\cite{helstrom1969quantum}, arbitrary qubit states with equal prior probabilities~\cite{deconinck2010qubit}, geometrically uniform states~\cite{eldar2004optimal}, and mirror-symmetric states~\cite{andersson2002minimum}.

In Chapter~\ref{ch:QSD}, we study this Bayes framework in a setting motivated by quantum computation. 
Rather than assuming that the candidate states are given as exponentially large classical vectors or matrices, we suppose that one has access to their \emph{source code}: quantum circuits that prepare the states. 
If the states have dimension $d$ and there are $L$ possible outcomes, the standard semidefinite programming formulation involves $L$ measurement operators acting on a $d$-dimensional Hilbert space, leading to a problem whose size scales with $d L$. 
This quickly becomes infeasible when the states are generated by many-qubit circuits.

We show that the corresponding semidefinite programs can be reformulated entirely in terms of the Gram matrix of the candidate states. 
This reduces the dimension of the optimization problem from one depending on the ambient Hilbert space dimension to one depending only on the number of candidate states and the number of possible outcomes. 
In particular, for $N$ candidate states and $L$ possible guesses, the reduced formulation has dimension $N L$, rather than $d L$. 
Moreover, the Gram matrix can be constructed by quantum pre-processing procedures that estimate inner products directly from the preparation circuits. 
This yields a hybrid quantum-classical method for Bayes-optimal quantum state discrimination that can operate directly on quantum data.

We apply this framework to two problems. 
The first is quantum changepoint identification~\cite{sentis2016quantum, sentis2017exact, sentis2018online} in which a quantum system changes from one state to another at one or more unknown locations, and the task is to identify the changepoint positions. 
Our framework allows us to study 
%multiple-changepoint regimes and 
reward functions that account for approximate localization, thereby capturing settings in which a nearly correct changepoint estimate should receive partial credit. 
The second application is quantum error type classification where the goal is to determine which error, from a known family of possible errors, has occurred, regardless of the exact position of this error. 
In this setting, the Gram matrix reduction makes the associated discrimination problems tractable for systems with hundreds of qubits.

Chapter~\ref{ch:PBM} focuses on a different aspect of state discrimination: the construction of simple measurements with provable performance guarantees. 
In the minimum-error setting, Bob uses the measurement with operators $\{M_1, \ldots, M_N\}$, where outcome $i$ corresponds to guessing that the state was $\rho_i$. 
His success probability is $\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}$, and the optimal value is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ch4_PBM-P_best}
    \Pbest = \max \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}.
\end{equation}
Recall that although this optimization can be solved numerically by semidefinite programming, the optimal measurement is difficult to characterize explicitly except in special cases. 
This motivates the study of measurements with a closed form that approximate the optimal one.

One such measurement is the \emph{pretty good measurement} (PGM), first discussed in~\cite{belavkin1975optimaldist, belavkin1975optimal, hughston1993complete, hausladen1994pretty}. 
The PGM has a simple closed form and is known to be optimal, or near optimal, in many structured settings. 
Examples include the hidden subgroup problem~\cite{bacon2005optimal, moore2005distinguishing}, quantum changepoint detection~\cite{sentis2016quantum}, and port-based teleportation~\cite{leditzky2022optimality}. 
More generally, the PGM often performs well for ensembles with a high degree of symmetry.

We then turn to the exclusion variant of state discrimination. 
Whereas discrimination asks Bob to identify which state was sent, exclusion asks him to name a state that was \emph{not} sent. 
Thus, if Alice sends $\rho_j$, Bob wins by outputting any index $i \neq j$. 
This task, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch4_PBM-fig2}, has been studied in connection with the Pusey-Barrett-Rudolph game~\cite{pusey2012reality} and related foundational questions~\cite{bandyopadhyay2014conclusive}. 
Perfect exclusion, also known as antidistinguishability, asks whether Bob can exclude a state without error; characterizing when this is possible remains an active area of research~\cite{caves2002quantum, havlivcek2020simple, russo2023inner, leifer2020noncontextuality, mishra2023optimal, uola2020all, johnston2023tight}.

In the exclusion setting, Bob seeks to minimize the probability of incorrectly excluding the state that was actually sent. 
If he constructs a measurement with operators $\{M_1, \ldots, M_N\}$, where outcome $j$ corresponds to excluding $\rho_j$, then the probability of an incorrect exclusion is $\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}$.
Thus, the optimal exclusion error probability is
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ch4_PBM-P_worst}
    \Pworst = \min \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}.    
\end{equation}
This is the opposite objective from minimum-error discrimination: the best exclusion measurement is, in this precise sense, the worst measurement for discrimination. 
Motivated by the role of the PGM as a well-studied approximation to the optimal discrimination measurement, we introduce the \emph{pretty bad measurement} as its analogue for state exclusion. 
We prove that the pretty good and pretty bad measurements each satisfy a natural baseline guarantee: they perform at least as well as blind guessing in their respective tasks.

Finally, in Chapter~\ref{ch:qseq}, we study state discrimination under severe memory constraints. 
%Up to this point, the most general measurements have been joint measurements, in which Bob may store all received quantum systems coherently and measure them together. 
When multiple quantum systems are received, the most general strategy is a joint measurement, in which Bob stores all systems coherently and measures them together.
Such measurements are mathematically optimal but may be experimentally demanding, especially for long sequences of states. 
This motivates the question of when simpler local or memoryless strategies can match, or approximate, the performance of optimal joint measurements.

Many quantum information-processing protocols naturally involve sequences of quantum states. 
For example, in the BB84 quantum key distribution protocol, Alice sends Bob qubits chosen from the set $\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{+}, \ket{-}\}$~\cite{bennett2014quantum}. 
From Bob's perspective, the received signal is a quantum sequence whose components are drawn from a known finite set. 
The resources required to optimally discriminate such sequences can vary significantly depending on the structure of the states and on the goal of the task. 
For two pure states given in finitely many copies, adaptive local strategies using limited classical memory can match the performance of optimal joint measurements, whereas memoryless strategies generally achieve this only asymptotically~\cite{acin2005multiple}. 
For two mixed states, joint measurements can strictly outperform all local strategies even in the asymptotic limit~\cite{calsamiglia2010local}. 
In quantum changepoint detection, joint measurements provide an advantage in the minimum-error setting~\cite{sentis2016quantum}, while in the unambiguous setting the optimality of local versus global strategies depends on the overlap between the states~\cite{sentis2017exact}. 
For symmetric pure-state identification, local strategies using only limited classical memory have also been shown to perform optimally in several cases~\cite{sentis2022online}.

Recent work has shown that, when the task is to identify an entire sequence of independently prepared states, memoryless strategies can achieve the same performance as joint measurements for both minimum-error and unambiguous discrimination~\cite{gupta2024unambiguous,gupta2024optimal}. 
This naturally raises a further question: does such an equivalence persist when Bob does not need to identify the entire sequence, but only a coarse-grained property of it?

Motivated by this question, we initiate a study of learning Boolean properties of quantum sequences. 
Suppose Bob receives a sequence
\begin{equation*}
    \ket{\psi_{x_1}} \otimes \ket{\psi_{x_2}} \otimes \cdots \otimes \ket{\psi_{x_n}},
\end{equation*}
where each $x_i \in \{0, 1\}$, and where $\ket{\psi_0}$ and $\ket{\psi_1}$ are known non-orthogonal states. 
Rather than learning the entire string $x = x_1 \cdots x_n$, Bob may only wish to determine a Boolean function $f(x)$. 
For example, he may want to decide whether the number of occurrences of one state exceeds the number of occurrences of the other, or whether the number of occurrences of $\ket{\psi_1}$ is even. 
These tasks are trivial when the classical string $x$ is directly available, but become non-trivial when $x$ is encoded into non-orthogonal quantum states.

We compare two resource regimes, illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ch1_global_vs_greedy}. 
In the \emph{full-memory} setting, Bob may store the entire sequence and perform an optimal joint measurement after all systems have been received. 
In the \emph{memoryless} setting, Bob has neither quantum nor classical memory: each state must be measured immediately using predetermined local measurements, and the final guess for $f(x)$ is computed from the resulting measurement outcomes. 
We characterize which Boolean functions can still be learned optimally under these stringent constraints. 
For functions outside this class, we give tight bounds showing how well such memoryless strategies can perform even when they are not optimal.

\paragraph{Pretty good measurement}

Consider the case where Bob wishes to maximize the average probability of correctly identifying the state $\rho_i$ that he received from Alice from the set $\{\rho_1, \ldots, \rho_N\}$ with associated probabilities $\{p_1, \ldots, p_N\}$. 
Suppose he measures with the measurements $\{M_1, \ldots, M_N\}$ and on outcome $j$ he guesses the state was $\rho_j$. 
Then, given state $\rho_j$, he guesses ``I think the state is $\rho_i$'' with probability 
$\inner{M_i}{\rho_j}$.  
In this case, he wins the game with probability 
%\begin{equation} 
$\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}$.  
%\end{equation} 
If Bob wishes to maximize this probability, then he is tasked with finding the best measurements $\{M_1, \dots, M_N\}$, known as the \emph{minimum error} measurement, and we define the best probability for Bob as 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ch4_PBM-P_best} 
    \Pbest = \text{maximize} \; \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}.
\end{equation} 

Given a problem instance, one can numerically solve for the best measurements using semidefinite programming~\cite{cvx}. 
Unfortunately, characterizing such POVMs is a difficult task known only to be possible in special cases, e.g., for $N=2$ the case of two states~\cite{helstrom1969quantum}, for $N$ arbitrary qubit states given with equal \emph{a priori} probabilities~\cite{deconinck2010qubit}, for geometrically uniform states~\cite{eldar2004optimal}, and mirror symmetric states~\cite{andersson2002minimum}.  
Therefore, it is of interest to find generic classes of measurements which have a simple form and \emph{approximate} $\Pbest$.

The pretty good measurement (PGM), first discussed in~\cite{belavkin1975optimaldist, belavkin1975optimal, hughston1993complete}, is a way to approximate the best possible measurement. 
The PGM is known to be optimal in certain cases, some nice examples being for the hidden subgroup problem~\cite{bacon2005optimal, moore2005distinguishing}, for the quantum change point problem~\cite{sentis2016quantum}, and in port-based teleportation~\cite{leditzky2022optimality}.  
Generally speaking, for problems with a large amount of symmetry, the PGM seems to perform well.

\paragraph{Quantum state exclusion}

We then turn to the exclusion variant of state discrimination. 
Whereas discrimination asks Bob to identify which state was sent, exclusion asks him to name a state that was not sent. 
This task can be viewed, in a precise sense, as optimizing the opposite objective from minimum-error discrimination. 
Motivated by the role of the \emph{pretty good measurement} as a simple and often effective approximation to the optimal discrimination measurement, we introduce the \emph{pretty bad measurement} as its analogue for exclusion. 
We prove that the pretty good and pretty bad measurements each satisfy a natural baseline guarantee: they perform at least as well as blind guessing in their respective objectives.

The quantum state \emph{exclusion} problem is similar to quantum state discrimination except that Bob wishes to guess a state \emph{which was not sent}. 
For example, if Alice sends $\rho_1$ to Bob and Bob responds with ``Alice, you did not send $\rho_3$'' then he would win the game in that case. 
The work in \cite{bandyopadhyay2014conclusive} studies this task in detail and its relationship to the PBR game~\cite{pusey2012reality}.

Unlike quantum state discrimination, it is much more difficult to determine when Bob has a perfect strategy to perfectly win this game, i.e., when he can exclude a state without error. 
If this is the case, we say that the states are \emph{antidistinguishable} which has been the topic of recent research~\cite{havlivcek2020simple, russo2023inner, leifer2020noncontextuality, mishra2023optimal, uola2020all, johnston2023tight}. 

If Bob wishes to find the measurement which best excludes a state, he wishes to minimize the probability of making an incorrect guess.
More precisely, if Bob measures $\rho_i$ with the measurements $\{M_1, \ldots, M_N\}$, he makes an \emph{incorrect guess} with probability $\inner{M_i}{\rho_i}$.  
Thus, he wishes to \emph{minimize} 
%\begin{equation} 
$\sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}$.  
%\end{equation} 

Notice that this is the exact opposite of what he wishes to do in the quantum state discrimination problem where he wants to maximize this quantity, see Eq.~\eqref{eq:ch4_PBM-P_best}. 
Thus, if Bob wants to exclude a state, his best measurement is also the \emph{worst} measurement for discrimination. 
Therefore, we define 
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:ch4_PBM-P_worst}
    \Pworst = \text{minimize} \; \sum_{i=1}^N p_i \inner{M_i}{\rho_i}. 
\end{equation}

It is worth noting that the viewpoint of Bob trying to lose the quantum state discrimination game is interesting in its own right. 
Indeed, Bob cannot always lose this game with certainty, even if he wants to, and understanding when this is the case, and more generally, understanding the above quantity gives insights into the very nature of quantum information.

Chapter~\ref{ch:PBM} focuses on constructing an analogue of the PGM for approximating $\Pworst$, which we call the \emph{pretty bad measurement}.

\paragraph{Memoryless regime}

Finally, we study what happens when it is not viable to implement the most general measurement strategy. 
Up to this point, we have considered joint measurements, which are the most general measurements one can perform on a collection of received quantum systems. 
Such measurements may require storing all systems coherently, which can be challenging experimentally. 
This motivates the question of whether joint measurements are always necessary, or whether simpler local strategies can still be optimal or near optimal when the states have additional structure. 
We investigate this question for the task of evaluating Boolean functions from sequences of quantum states. 
We compare two contrasting resource regimes. 
In the \emph{full-memory} setting, the entire finite sequence may be stored and an optimal joint measurement may be performed after all states have been received. 
In the \emph{memoryless} setting, neither quantum nor classical memory is available, and each state must be measured immediately using predetermined local measurements. 
We characterize which binary properties can still be learned optimally under these stringent constraints, and show that even when not optimal, they still perform well.

Suppose now that Bob is promised that every one of the candidate states is a sequence of the form
\begin{equation}
    \ket{\psi} \otimes \ket{\phi} \otimes \ket{\psi} \otimes \cdots \otimes \ket{\psi},
\end{equation}
where $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}$ are known to him.
While each position is known to be either $\ket{\psi}$ or $\ket{\phi}$, the identity of each state is unknown.
Can Bob determine whether the number of $\ket{\psi}$ states exceeds the number of $\ket{\phi}$ states, or whether the total number of $\ket{\psi}$ states is even?
These examples highlight a basic task: learning coarse-grained properties of a quantum sequence without necessarily determining the sequence itself.

We study this problem in two contrasting resource regimes as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:ch1_global_vs_greedy}.
In the \emph{full-memory} setting the entire (finite) sequence may be stored so that an optimal joint measurement can be performed upon receiving all of the states.
In the \emph{memoryless} setting, neither quantum nor classical memory is available and each state must be measured immediately, using only predetermined local measurements.
Here, we investigate which binary properties (properties which can be either true or false) can still be learned optimally under such stringent memory constraints.

Many information-processing and quantum-computing protocols involve discriminating sequences of quantum states.
The well-known BB84 quantum key distribution scheme is an example where Alice sends Bob qubits chosen at random from the set $\{\ket{0}, \ket{1}, \ket{+}, \ket{-}\}$ \cite{bennett2014quantum}.
From Bob's perspective, the received signal is a quantum sequence whose individual components are drawn from this set.
The central question in such scenarios concerns the resources required to optimally discriminate the given sequence, which might vary significantly across different sequence types.
For example, when discriminating two pure states given a \emph{finite number of copies}, adaptive local strategies that make limited use of classical memory to condition later measurements based on earlier outcomes achieve the same performance as optimal joint measurements, whereas memoryless strategies generally do so only asymptotically \cite{acin2005multiple}.
However, for two mixed states, joint measurements can strictly outperform all local strategies even in the asymptotic limit \cite{calsamiglia2010local}.
In the quantum change-point detection problem, joint measurements provide an advantage in the minimum-error paradigm \cite{sentis2016quantum}, while for unambiguous detection the optimality of local versus global strategies depends on the overlap between the two states \cite{sentis2017exact}.
For identification of symmetric pure states, local strategies have been proposed that often achieve optimality using classical memory to store only the last obtained measurement outcome \cite{sentis2022online}.

More recently, for problems where the goal is to identify the entire sequence composed of independently prepared states, it has been shown that memoryless strategies are optimal in the sense that they achieve the same performance as joint measurements, for both minimum-error and unambiguous discrimination \cite{gupta2024unambiguous,gupta2024optimal}.
This observation naturally raises the question of whether such equivalence persists when the discrimination task seeks to learn only specific properties or coarse-grained features of the sequence, rather than its exact identity.

Motivated by this, we initiate a study of learning Boolean properties of quantum sequences.
In a classical information-processing setup, data may be represented by bit strings, and computing Boolean functions of these strings, such as the $\XOR$, is trivial; the main challenges lie instead in algorithmic efficiency and robustness of protocols.
In the quantum setting, by contrast, computing even a simple function becomes non-trivial when only quantum encodings of the data is available.
We consider which properties can be optimally learned using memoryless measurement strategies, and which fundamentally require joint measurements across the sequence.
The paradigm we focus on is minimum-error, where the learner might obtain an incorrect value for the property of the sequence and their goal is to minimize this error, and we completely characterize the Boolean functions for which a class of fixed local measurements are optimal, and give tightness of bounds when they are not.

\subsection{Notations}

A \emph{complex Euclidean space}, denoted by $\C^n$, is an $n$-dimensional vector space over the field of complex numbers $\C$ equipped with the inner product $\inner{u}{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n \bar{u}_i v_i$ for $u, v \in \C^n$, where $\bar{u}_i$ denotes the complex conjugate of $u_i$.
We denote complex Euclidean spaces by letters in calligraphic font, e.g., $\A, \B, \X$ and $\Y$, and use the shorthand $[n]$ for $\{ 1, \ldots, n \}$.
The set of all $n$-bit strings is denoted by $\{0,1\}^n$.

A \emph{Boolean function} on $n$ variables is a function $f: \{0,1\}^n \to \{0,1\}$.
In Chapter~\ref{ch:qseq}, we consider the $\AND$ function and the $\MAJ$ function. 
The $\AND$ function on $n$ bits is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \AND(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = x_1 \wedge x_2 \wedge \cdots \wedge x_n = \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if } x_i = 1 \text{ for all } i, \\
        0 & \text{otherwise}.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
For odd $n = 2k+1$, the majority function is defined by
\begin{equation}
    \MAJ(x_1,\ldots,x_{2k+1}) = \begin{cases}
        1 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^{2k+1} x_i > k, \\
        0 & \text{if } \sum_{i=1}^{2k+1} x_i \leq k.
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

A Boolean function is said to be \emph{affine} if it can be written as $f(x) = \bigoplus_{i=1}^n a_i x_i \oplus b$, where $a_1, \ldots, a_n, b \in \{0,1\}$ and $\oplus$ denotes addition modulo $2$.
For strings $x, y \in \{0,1\}^n$, the \emph{Hamming distance} between $x$ and $y$, denoted by $d_H(x,y)$, is the number of bit positions in which they differ. 
The \emph{Hamming weight} of a string $x \in \{0, 1\}^n$, denoted by $|x|$, is the number of ones in $x$, equivalently $|x|=\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$.

For integers $n$ and $k$ satisfying $0 \leq k \leq n$, the \emph{binomial coefficient} is defined as $\binom{n}{k}=\frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}$.
It counts the number of ways to choose $k$ elements from a set of size $n$. 
Equivalently, it is the number of strings in $\{0, 1\}^n$ with Hamming weight $k$.

A random variable $X$ is said to have a \emph{Bernoulli distribution} with parameter $p \in [0,1]$, written $X \sim \Bern(p)$, if $X$ takes values in $\{0, 1\}$ with
\begin{equation}
    \Pr[X=1]=p \qquad \text{and} \qquad \Pr[X=0]=1-p.
\end{equation}
A random variable $Y$ is said to have a \emph{binomial distribution} with parameters $n$ and $p$, written $Y \sim \Binom(n,p)$,
if $Y$ is the sum of $n$ independent Bernoulli random variables, each with parameter $p$. 
Equivalently,
\begin{equation}
    \Pr[Y=k]=\binom{n}{k} p^k (1-p)^{n-k}    
\end{equation}
for each $k \in \{0, \ldots, n\}$.

\subsection{Linear algebra}

The $p$-\emph{norm} for a vector $u \in \C^n$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \|u\|_p = \left( \sum_{i=1}^n |u_i|^p \right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
\end{equation}
for $p \geq 1$ and
\begin{equation}
    \|u\|_\infty = \max \{ |u_i|\ :\ i \in [n] \}.
\end{equation}

For a matrix $A$, we define its \emph{adjoint} as the matrix $A^*$ which is computed as $A^* = \overline{A^\top}$ where $A^\top$ is the transpose of $A$ and $\bar{A}$ is the entry-wise complex conjugate of $A$.

For matrices
\begin{equation*}
    A = \begin{pmatrix}
        a_{11} & \cdots & a_{1n} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        a_{m1} & \cdots & a_{mn}
    \end{pmatrix} \in \C^{m \times n}
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    B = \begin{pmatrix}
        b_{11} & \cdots & b_{1q} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        b_{p1} & \cdots & b_{pq}
    \end{pmatrix} \in \C^{p \times q},
\end{equation*}
we define their \emph{tensor product} as
\begin{equation}
    A \otimes B = \begin{pmatrix}
        a_{11} B & \cdots & a_{1n} B \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        a_{m1} B & \cdots & a_{mn} B
    \end{pmatrix} \in \C^{mp \times nq}.
\end{equation}

The \emph{trace} of $A \in \C^{n \times n}$ is defined as $\tr(A) = \sum_{i=1}^n a_{ii}$.
This function satisfies $\tr(AB) = \tr(BA)$ for all $A \in \C^{m \times n}$ and $B \in \C^{n \times m}$, which is referred to as the \emph{cyclic property} of trace.
The trace can be used to define an inner product as $\inner{A}{B} = \tr(A^* B)$ for all $A, B \in \C^{m \times n}$, called the \emph{Hilbert-Schmidt inner product}.

A matrix $A \in \C^{n \times n}$ is said to be \emph{Hermitian} if it satisfies $A = A^*$.
We denote the set of all Hermitian matrices on $\X$ as $\Herm(\X)$.
The eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix are real and thus can be ordered in either increasing or decreasing order.
We denote the largest and smallest eigenvalue of a matrix $A$ by $\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(A)$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}(A)$ respectively.

A Hermitian matrix $B \in \C^{n \times n}$ is said to be \emph{positive semidefinite} if it admits the decomposition $B = A^* A$ for some $A \in \C^{n \times n}$.
The set of all positive semidefinite matrices on $\X$ is denoted as $\Pos(\X)$.
The eigenvalues of a positive semidefinite matrix are non-negative, and we write $B \succcurlyeq 0$ to indicate that a matrix $B$ is positive semidefinite. 
More generally, for matrices $A, B \in \Herm(\X)$, the notation $A \succcurlyeq B$ means that $A-B$ is positive semidefinite.
For a positive semidefinite matrix $B$, its \emph{square root} is the unique matrix $Y \succcurlyeq 0$ satisfying $Y^2 = B$, and we denote it as $B^{1/2}$.

A matrix $X \in \C^{n \times n}$ is \emph{invertible} if there exists a matrix $Y \in \C^{n \times n}$ such that $YX = \id$.
When such a $Y$ exists it is denoted by $X^{-1}$.
Since $X$ is square, we also have that $X X^{-1} = \id$.
The \emph{Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse} of $X$ is the unique operator $X^+$ satisfying:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $X\ X^+\ X = X$,
    \item $X^+\ X\ X^+ = X^+$, and
    \item $X\ X^+$ and $X^+\ X$ are both Hermitian.
\end{enumerate}
When $X$ is invertible, $X^+ = X^{-1}$.

A matrix $U \in \C^{n \times n}$ is a \emph{unitary} operator if it preserves the Euclidean norm, i.e., $\|Ux\| = \|x\|$ for all $x \in \C^n$.
These matrices preserve inner products as well, i.e., $\inner{Ux}{Uy} = \inner{x}{y}$ for all $x, y \in \C^n$.
They satisfy the equation $U^* U = U U^* = \id$ and are invertible.

For a matrix $A \in \C^{m \times n}$ and a real number $p \geq 1$, we define the \emph{Schatten $p$-norm} as
\begin{equation}
    \| A \|_p = \left( \tr((A^* A)^\frac{p}{2}) \right)^\frac{1}{p}.
\end{equation}

The Schatten $1$-norm is also referred to as the \emph{trace} norm and the Schatten $2$-norm is referred to as the \emph{Frobenius} norm.

%The Frobenius norm is defined as the norm that this inner product induces, i.e., 
%\begin{equation} 
%\| X \|_F = \sqrt{\tr(X^2)}. 
%\end{equation}
The Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for Hermitian matrices is
\begin{equation} 
|\inner{X}{Y}| \leq \| X \|_F \| Y \|_F 
\end{equation} 
with equality if and only if $X$ and $Y$ are linearly dependent. 
Therefore, if \emph{nonzero} Hermitian $X$ and $Y$ satisfy 
\begin{equation} 
\inner{X}{Y} = \| X \|_F \| Y \|_F 
\end{equation} 
then there must exist $\lambda > 0$ such that $X = \lambda Y$.

Let $\A$ and $\B$ be complex Euclidean spaces. 
The \emph{adjoint} of a linear map $\Phi : \A \to \B$ is the unique linear map $\Phi^* : \B \to \A$ that satisfies $\inner{\Phi(A)}{B} = \inner{A}{\Phi^*(B)}$ for all $A$ in $\A$ and $B$ in $\B$.
The \emph{identity map} on operators on $\A$, denoted by $\idch_\A$, maps every operator on $\A$ to itself. 
The \emph{partial trace} over $\A$, denoted by $\tr_\A$, is defined as $\tr_\A(A\ \otimes\ B)=\tr(A)\ B$ for every operator $A$ on $\A$ and every operator $B$ on $\B$, and extended to all operators by linearity.
Finally, a linear map $\Xi$ from operators on $\A$ to operators on $\B$ is \emph{Hermiticity-preserving} if $\Xi(A)$ is Hermitian for every Hermitian operator $A$ on $\A$.

The \emph{Gram matrix} $G$ of a set of vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_N\}$ is the Hermitian positive semidefinite matrix whose entries are given by $G_{ij} = \inner{v_i}{v_j}$.

An $N \times N$ \emph{Toeplitz matrix} $T$ is a matrix with entries satisfying  
\begin{equation}
    T_{i,\ j} = T_{i + 1,\ j + 1} \text{ for all } i,\ j \in [N-1].
\end{equation}

A Toeplitz matrix can be written as
\begin{align*}
    T = 
        \begin{pmatrix}
            t_0 & t_{-1} & t_{-2} & \cdots & t_{-(N - 1)} \\
            t_1 & t_0 & t_{-1} & \cdots & t_{-(N - 2)} \\
            t_2 & t_1 & t_0 & \cdots & t_{-(N - 3)} \\
            \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
            t_{N - 1} & t_{N - 2} & t_{N - 3} & \cdots & t_0
        \end{pmatrix},
\end{align*}
noting that every diagonal (main, sub and super) has constant entries.
This property lets us alternately express $T$ as
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:Toep_mat}
    T = \sum_{k = -(N-1)}^{N-1} t_k \Theta_{k},
\end{align}
where $\Theta_k$ is the $N \times N$ Toeplitz matrix with ones on the $k$-th diagonal and zeros elsewhere.
Here, $k = 0$ indicates the principal diagonal, $k > 0$ denotes the sub-diagonals, and $k < 0$ corresponds to the super-diagonals.
%Note that a Toeplitz matrix is not necessarily square.

A set $\mathcal{C}$ is \emph{convex} if we have $\lambda x + (1 - \lambda) y \in \mathcal{C}$, for all $x, y \in \mathcal{C}$ and $\lambda \in [0, 1]$.
A set $\K$ is a \emph{cone} if $\lambda x \in \K$ for all $x \in \K$ and $\lambda \geq 0$.
A cone that is also convex is referred to as a \emph{convex cone}.
%We say that $\K$ is a convex cone if it is convex and also closed under nonnegative scaling, meaning that if $x \in \K$ then $\lambda x \in \K$ for all $\lambda \geq 0$. 
\emph{Cone programming} is the study of optimizing a linear function over a variable in a convex cone $\K$ subject to affine constraints. 
In standard form, we can write a cone program as 
\begin{equation} 
    \text{maximize } \Big\{ \inner{X}{A} : \Xi(X) = B, \; X \in \K \Big\} 
\end{equation} 
where $\Xi$ is a linear map.
The \emph{convex hull} of a set $S$, denoted $\conv(S)$, is the intersection of all convex sets containing $S$.

An order-$r$ \emph{Krylov subspace} generated by an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ and an $n$-dimensional vector $b$ is the set of vectors
\begin{equation*}
    \K_r(A, b) = \mathrm{span} \left\{ b, A b, A^2 b, \ldots, A^{r-1} b \right\}.
\end{equation*}
Let $r_0 = \dim (\mathrm{span} \left\{ b, A b, A^2 b, \ldots \right\})$.
We have that $\left\{ b, A b, A^2 b, \ldots, A^{r-1} b \right\}$ are linearly independent unless $r > r_0$.



\subsection{Quantum information}

A \emph{density operator} is a positive semidefinite operator with unit trace. 
We denote the set of all density operators on $\X$ by $\mathrm{D}(\X)$. 
A \emph{quantum state}, often referred to simply as a \emph{state}, is represented by a density operator.

We use Dirac notation, where $\ket{\psi}$ denotes a vector in a complex Euclidean space and $\bra{\psi}$ denotes its adjoint. 
For vectors $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}$, the inner product is written as $\braket{\psi}{\phi}$, and their outer product is expressed as $\ketbra{\psi}{\phi}$.
We denote  the standard basis of $\C^n$ by $\ket{0}, \ldots, \ket{n-1}$.

Consider states $\{\rho_1, \ldots, \rho_n\}$ with associated probabilities $\{p_1, \ldots, p_n\}$. 
The collection $\{(p_i,\rho_i): i \in [n]\}$ is called an \emph{ensemble} of states, and the state $\rho = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i \rho_i$
is called the corresponding \emph{mixture}. 
A rank-one state is called a \emph{pure state}.

A state $\rho \in \mathrm{D}(\X_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \X_n)$ is said to be a \emph{product state} if it can be expressed as
$\rho = \sigma_1 \otimes \cdots \otimes \sigma_n$, where $\sigma_i \in \mathrm{D}(\X_i)$ for each $i \in [n]$. 
A state is called \emph{separable} if it can be expressed as a convex combination of product states; otherwise, it is called \emph{entangled}.

The \emph{maximally mixed state} on a complex Euclidean space $\X$ is the state $\frac{1}{\dim(\X)} \id_\X$.
The \emph{uniform superposition state} in $\X = \mathbb{C}^n$ is defined as $\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \ket{i}$.

A \emph{measurement} is described by a positive operator-valued measure, or POVM, which is a collection of operators $M_i$ for $i \in [n]$ satisfying $M_i \succcurlyeq 0$ for each $i$, and $\sum_{i = 1}^n M_i = \id$.
The operators $M_i$ are referred to as the measurement operators, and each $i \in [n]$ indexes the possible measurement outcomes. 
If the system is in state $\rho \in \mathrm{D}(\X)$, then the probability of obtaining the outcome $i$ upon measuring with $M_i$ is given by $\inner{M_i}{\rho}$.
This expression for the outcome probabilities is known as \emph{Born's rule}.

\subsection{Overlap estimation in quantum computing}\label{sec:QC_bg} 

\paragraph{Swap test.}

Given two (possibly unknown) pure states $\ket{\psi}$ and $\ket{\phi}$, the swap test outputs $0$ with probability $\frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}|\braket{\psi}{\phi}|^2$.
Figure~\ref{fig:swap_test} depicts the corresponding circuit.
One can learn $|\braket{\psi}{\phi}|^2$ to additive accuracy $\epsilon$ with failure probability at most $\delta$ using $\Ocomp \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \log \left( \frac{1}{\delta} \right) \right)$ copies of the states and $\tilde{\Ocomp} \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \log \left( \frac{1}{\delta} \right) \right)$ operations~\cite{buhrman2001quantum,huang2019near}.

\paragraph{Hadamard test.}

Given access to a controlled unitary $U$ that maps the state $\ket{\psi}$ to the state $\ket{\phi}$, this method creates a random variable whose expected value is the real part of $\braket{\psi}{\phi}$.
Figure~\ref{fig:hadamard_test} illustrates the circuit for this procedure.
The imaginary part of $\braket{\psi}{\phi}$ can be computed by applying a phase gate $S$ (up to a known sign) on the first qubit before the controlled unitary operation.
We can learn $\braket{\psi}{\phi}$ to additive accuracy $\epsilon$ with failure probability at most $\delta$ using $\Ocomp \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \log \left( \frac{1}{\delta} \right) \right)$ copies of the states and $\tilde{\Ocomp} \left(\frac{1}{\epsilon^2} \log \left( \frac{1}{\delta} \right) \right)$ operations~\cite{aharonov2006polynomial,huang2019near}.

\subsection{Semidefinite programming} \label{ss:ch1_intro-SDP}

Semidefinite programming is an area of convex optimization where the goal is to optimize a linear function of a positive semidefinite matrix $X$ over affine constraints. 
A semidefinite program (SDP) can be written in standard form
    \begin{equation*}    
        \begin{aligned}
            \alpha = \inf \quad & \inner{C}{X} \\
            \suchthat \quad & \inner{A_i}{X} = b_i,\ i \in [m] \\
            & \hspace{30pt} X \succcurlyeq 0,
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation*}
where the matrices $C$ and $A_i$ are Hermitian and $b_i$ are real, and $\inf$ is short for infimum. 
Every SDP has a dual which is an SDP itself and is defined as 
    \begin{equation}
        \label{eq:power_rangers_SPD}
        \begin{aligned}
            \beta = \sup \quad & \inner{b}{y} \\
            \suchthat \quad & \sum_{i=1}^m y_i A_i \preccurlyeq C \\
            & \hspace{35pt} y \in \mathbb{R}^m,  
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}     
where $\sup$ is short for supremum.
We have that $\alpha \geq \beta$, a fact known as weak duality. 
The equality $\alpha = \beta$ is known as strong duality. 
It does not hold in general, but Slater's condition provides a sufficient criterion: if the primal is strictly feasible (there exists a feasible $X \succ 0$) and $\alpha$ is finite, then $\alpha = \beta$ and the dual optimum is attained (and symmetrically with the roles exchanged)~\cite{boyd2004convex}.
%If both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are finite and one has a feasible solution where the inequalities are strict (known as strict feasibility), then $\alpha = \beta$. 
%This condition is known as strong duality.

%The above optimization in this case is called \emph{semidefinite programming} and is of great interest in many areas including combinatorial optimization~\cite{tunccel2016polyhedral} and quantum information~\cite{watrous2018theory} and we refer to a specific instance as a semidefinite program. 
Semidefinite programming is of great interest in many areas including combinatorial optimization~\cite{tunccel2016polyhedral} and quantum information~\cite{watrous2018theory}.
While optimizing over separable operators is NP-hard~\cite{gurvits2004classical, gharibian2010strong}, one can often solve SDPs to any fixed accuracy in polynomial time under mild conditions.
Modeling frameworks such as CVX~\cite{grant2014cvx}, together with solvers such as SeDuMi~\cite{sturm1999using}, SDPT3~\cite{toh1999sdpt3} and Mosek~\cite{andersen2000mosek} make such problems straightforward to specify and solve in practice. 
Since SDPs can often be solved efficiently, they are a popular choice for approximating convex sets which are difficult to deal with.%, a great example being $\Sep$.
