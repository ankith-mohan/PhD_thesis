Many important questions in quantum information theory can be viewed as optimization problems: how to identify an unknown quantum signal, how much entanglement a quantum system contains, or how to design measurements that extract the desired information from quantum data. In principle, many of these questions can be expressed using semidefinite programming, a powerful form of convex optimization. In practice, however, two major obstacles arise. First, the dimension of a quantum system grows exponentially with the number of qubits. Second, physically meaningful constraints, such as requiring a state to be separable, or unentangled, can make the resulting optimization problems fundamentally hard.

This thesis develops dimension-reduction methods for several high-dimensional optimization problems in quantum information. The central theme is that many apparently intractable quantum optimization tasks contain hidden structure: the full exponentially large quantum description is often not needed, and the essential information can sometimes be captured by a smaller object. In several cases, this smaller object can be obtained using a hybrid quantum–classical procedure, where quantum subroutines estimate the quantities needed to define a reduced classical optimization problem.

The first part of the thesis studies optimization over separable quantum states. Separability testing is computationally hard, and standard semidefinite programming hierarchies quickly become impractical as the number of qubits increases. We introduce a hybrid quantum–classical method that uses a quantum co-processor to reduce the effective dimension of the optimization problem. A key feature of the method is that it always outputs a valid separable state, rather than only producing a bound on the optimum. We apply this approach to estimating the minimum separable energy of many-body Hamiltonians, which in turn defines a measure of how entangled their ground spaces are. Numerical experiments demonstrate scalability to systems with up to 28 qubits.

The second part develops a general framework for quantum state discrimination using Helstrom’s general Bayes approach. This framework allows one to describe many discrimination tasks through a reward or penalty rule, including minimum-error discrimination, unambiguous discrimination, state exclusion, and more flexible objectives where near-misses or inconclusive answers receive partial credit. We show that the corresponding semidefinite program can be reformulated using only the Gram matrix of the candidate states. This changes the size of the optimization problem from one depending on the Hilbert-space dimension to one depending primarily on the number of candidate states and possible guesses. When the states are given through their preparation circuits, this Gram matrix can be estimated using quantum overlap-estimation procedures, yielding a hybrid quantum–classical algorithm that operates directly from quantum source data.

This state discrimination framework is then applied to two structured problems. The first is quantum changepoint identification, where the task is to determine where one or more changes occur in a sequence of quantum states. The reduced formulation makes it possible to study several reward structures, including objectives that reward guesses that are close to the true changepoint, and it enables computations in multiple-changepoint regimes that are inaccessible to the naive formulation. The second application is quantum error type classification, where an n-qubit state is affected by a single-qubit Pauli error and the goal is to identify the type of error, rather than the exact qubit on which it occurred. The Gram-matrix reduction makes this classification problem tractable for systems with hundreds of qubits in structured examples.

The thesis also studies simple and structured measurement strategies. As a counterpart to the well-known pretty good measurement for minimum-error discrimination, we introduce the pretty bad measurement for state exclusion. We prove that both strategies perform at least as well as blind guessing in their respective settings. Finally, we consider the problem of evaluating Boolean functions from a sequence of quantum systems under severe memory constraints. We prove that a simple local greedy measurement strategy is globally optimal exactly for affine Boolean functions, and more generally achieves at least the square of the optimal global success probability.

Overall, this thesis shows that high-dimensional quantum optimization problems often admit principled reductions that preserve the essential structure of the task. By combining dimension reduction, hybrid quantum–classical computation, and provable performance guarantees, the results make it possible to analyze quantum discrimination, separability, classification, and measurement-design problems at scales that would be out of reach for direct methods.
